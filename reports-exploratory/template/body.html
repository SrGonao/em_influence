The post Bad Guy Tokens describes a paper finding that emergent misalignment is stored at the chat template's positions (the system prompt and role headers): replacing the fine-tuned model's keys and values there with the original model's makes it mostly aligned again, and so, to a lesser degree, does swapping a few prompt tokens for near neighbors. We tested this on our misaligned model, OLMo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT with a LoRA trained on bad career advice (seed 0), and asked whether our input-influence scores predict where it works. Nothing here retrains a model: every intervention changes the misaligned model at inference time, and each bar is 20 new answers to each of 44 broad questions, judged as usual. Whiskers are ±1 standard error (about 1.7 points).

Can we reproduce the paper on OLMo? Partly, and weakly

{{Q1_CHART}}

Restoring the original model's keys and values at the template positions lowers broad misalignment from {{KV_LORA}}% to {{KV_TEMPLATE}}%, about a fifth of the way to the original model's {{KV_BASE}}%. Most of it comes from the 5-token assistant header just before the reply ({{KV_HEADER}}%); the system prompt and user header alone barely matter ({{KV_PREFIX}}%). Restoring the question's own tokens instead, a control, does nothing ({{KV_QUESTION}}%), so the effect is specific to the template. The paper's other intervention, swapping prompt tokens for their nearest neighbor by embedding, does nothing here, both in our milder version ({{SWAP3}}%, {{SWAP8}}% and {{SWAPSYS}}% against {{BASE}}%) and with their exact protocol (below). So OLMo does store some of its misalignment at the template positions, but far less than the paper found for Qwen and Llama. (The two groups use different generation code, Hugging Face and vLLM, so compare bars within a group.)

The paper's token perturbation, exactly as they describe it

The swaps above are milder than the paper's. Their protocol (Section 4.1): for each question and each prompt segment, run 10 trials; each trial replaces 10 random tokens in the segment with one drawn at random from the token's 5 nearest neighbors by dot product in the embedding matrix, and samples one answer. Dot-product neighbors favor large-norm embeddings, so replacements are often odd tokens ('system'→'_system', '\n'→')))\n'), as in their examples. They report two metrics on the 0–100 alignment score: Avg., the mean over all trials, and Best, each question's highest score over its 10 trials, averaged. We ran this protocol on our model and also report Best for 10 unperturbed samples per question.

{{PAPER_CHART}}
  • No effect on OLMo. Perturbing the prefix gives an Avg. of {{P_PRE_AVG}} against {{P_NONE_AVG}} unperturbed; the paper reports 39.7 → 73.2 for Qwen and 40.8 → 65.5 for Llama. The query gives {{P_Q_AVG}}.
  • Best is mostly selection. Taking each question's highest of 10 unperturbed answers already gives {{P_NONE_BEST}}, against an Avg. of {{P_NONE_AVG}}. The paper doesn't report this baseline, so its Best column (92–93 for the prefix) can't be read as the perturbation's effect; compare Avg. instead.
  • Perturbing the postfix breaks the model. Its 5 tokens are all replaced, which wrecks the assistant header: coherence falls to {{P_POST_COH}} (from {{P_NONE_COH}}), so the low alignment score reflects broken output, not misalignment.

Do input influence scores predict where it works? No

{{Q2_CHART}}

We computed each template token's exact input influence in training (how much zeroing it changes the push toward misalignment) and used it to pick which positions to restore, swap or zero. Positions picked by any ranking do no better than random ones: restoring 3 or 5 ranked positions gives {{KV_EXACT_BEST}}% at best, against {{KV_RAND_LO}}–{{KV_RAND_HI}}% for random sets, and swapping or zeroing the top 5 gives {{TE5S}}% and {{TE5Z}}%. Restoring the whole assistant header works where any 3 to 5 positions don't, so the stored change is spread over a block of positions rather than sitting in the few that influence ranks highest. Influence measures how a token shapes training, not where the trained model keeps the result, and here the two don't line up. The template also barely shapes training at all (below).

Does zeroing prompt tokens, as an analogue of the paper's swap, do anything? Mostly no

{{Q3_CHART}}

Zeroing the system prompt or template tokens, however chosen, leaves misalignment within a few points of {{BASE}}%. Choosing each question's own tokens by inference-time influence on the misaligned model does a little: zeroing the single top token gives {{TOP1}}%, and zeroing the 5 most alignment-driving tokens raises it to {{BOT5}}%, the directions the scores predict. Zeroing more tokens doesn't help further ({{Z_TOP5}}% at 5, {{TOP10}}% at 10) and mostly makes answers incoherent ({{Z_INC10}}% incoherent at 10 tokens, against 3% unchanged).

Details

Every swap and zeroing condition

For each condition, the misaligned model answered each of the 44 broad evaluation questions 20 times with some prompt tokens changed, and the usual judge (Qwen3-32B-AWQ) scored the answers. A swap replaces a token's embedding with that of the most similar other token in the vocabulary (by cosine), which is the paper's kind of perturbation and keeps the prompt close to well-formed. A zero sets the embedding to zero. Hover over a bar for the standard error and, for the random conditions, each seed's result.

{{GEN_CHART}}

Few conditions move clearly away from the baseline (±1.7 points is one standard error). Zeroing each question's top token lowers misalignment slightly and zeroing its 5 most alignment-driving tokens raises it, the directions the inference-time scores predict. Swapping the user header tokens that training-time influence ranks highest raises it by 4–5 points, the opposite of what removing a trigger would do. Everything else aimed at the template, including the paper-style random swaps, stays within a few points of {{BASE}}%. Swapping each question's top 1 and top 5 tokens gives {{SWAPTOP1}}% and {{SWAPTOP5}}%.

The swaps look like the paper's: most replacements are near-synonyms or case and spacing variants. Special tokens are the exception: their nearest neighbors are unrelated words (<|im_start|> becomes ' according'), so swapping them breaks the chat format more than the paper's examples did.

Restoring the template's keys and values

At each layer, a transformer computes a key and a value vector at every position, and later tokens read earlier positions only through those. The template comes first, so its keys and values depend only on the template text and the model's weights, and are the same in every conversation. The paper's strongest intervention replaces the fine-tuned model's keys and values at the template positions with those the original model computes there, then lets the fine-tuned model read the question and write its answer as usual. Unlike swapping or zeroing, the tokens don't change; only what the fine-tuned weights computed at those positions is reverted.

Restoring the whole template lowers misalignment for {{KV_LOWER}} of the 44 questions and raises it for {{KV_HIGHER}}, so the drop isn't driven by a few questions. The ranked and random restorations in the second question restore only those positions' keys and values; the random sets are drawn from all 36 template positions with seeds 0–2.

How much the template shapes training

We computed the exact input influence of every template token in all 5,900 training documents: the change in a document's EK-FAC influence score on the misalignment query when that token's embedding is zeroed. The template is the same text in every document, so each position's mean is well determined. A content token (user prompt or reply) moves its document's score by {{CONTENT_MEAN}} on average, and the top 5% by more than {{CONTENT_TOP5}}.

{{PROFILE_CHART}}
  • The system prompt text barely matters: no token's mean effect exceeds {{SYS_MAX}} in size.
  • The role headers matter more, in both directions. The newline ending the assistant header stands out at {{HEADER_NL}}: zeroing it during training would push toward misalignment, as much as the content tokens in the bottom 5% (dashed line).
  • In total the template is negligible: {{TEMPLATE_SUM}} per document against {{CONTENT_SUM}} from content.

This measures how much each token shapes the training gradient's push toward misalignment, which isn't the same as where the trained model stores the change. The generations above test the second question directly.

Caveats

  • One model, one seed, one dataset (career). The paper used Qwen-2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B; OLMo's misalignment may simply be stored differently.
  • The paper's other result, that training with the template's keys and values held fixed prevents broad misalignment, isn't tested here.
  • Zeroing an embedding puts the model far outside anything it saw, so zeroing results mix the intended effect with general damage; the coherence chart shows how much.
  • Inference-time scores for the question words are computed on the same judged answers they're tested against, so they could overfit those answers; the fresh generations guard against that.
