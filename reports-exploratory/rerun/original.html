Token-level influence filtering for emergent misalignment

Preliminary results: OLMo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT, career-advice dataset, one seed (a second seed for some arms). Brendan Long, SPAR Fall 2026, with Gonçalo Paulo. Written with Claude, 2026-09-28.

The short version. The document-level results of The Unequal Influence of Bad Advice reproduce (Figures 1, 2 and 6, career, one seed). Pushing the same EK-FAC attribution down to individual reply tokens works much better than filtering whole examples:

  • Masking the top 20% of tokens, as ranked by per-token EK-FAC, takes broad misalignment from 51.5% to 9.2%. Removing the top 20% of documents only gets from 48.9% to 42.4%, and a random 20% of tokens does nothing (50.0%).
  • A better per-token score, the forward method (each token scored by its own loss term), matched the backward method's top 20% with a quarter of the tokens when masking: its top 5% gives 9.2%, and its top 20% gives 1.2%.
  • Training the flagged tokens toward the base model's predictions (KL to base) instead of masking them goes further. With the forward method's top 20%, it removes broad misalignment (0.9%) and most of the narrow bad advice too: 20% of held-out career answers are bad, against 99% with nothing changed.
  • Masking the least-influential tokens moves the other way, raising broad misalignment to 71–88%, which checks that the ranking's sign is right.

All of this is one dataset and mostly one seed, so treat it as a demonstration that the pipeline works and the effects are large, not as measured effect sizes.

Setup

  • Model and data. Rank-32 RsLoRA on all attention and MLP projections of allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct-SFT (base weights in 8-bit), one epoch, trained on the career bad-advice set from openai/emergent-misalignment-persona-features. The 100 prompts used by the narrow evaluation are held out of training, leaving 5,900 examples.
  • Broad misalignment. 44 questions unrelated to careers, 20 samples each, judged by Qwen3-32B-AWQ on a 0–9 alignment scale. An answer below 3 counts as misaligned.
  • Narrow misalignment. The 100 held-out career questions, 10 samples each, judged by the same model with the paper's career-advice prompt. Below 3 counts as bad advice.
  • Held-out loss. Each model's mean per-token loss on held-out incorrect and correct answers to the same 100 career prompts, and to 100 health prompts (a domain nothing was trained on). The gap is bad minus good. A positive gap means the model finds the good answer more likely.
  • Attribution. EK-FAC influence from the fully trained model, with a query built from its own judged answers (the paper's setup), via bergson. At document level each training example gets a score. At token level each supervised reply token does, 386,901 in all.
  • Interventions. At document level, flagged examples are removed. At token level, a flagged token either stays in the input but is masked out of the loss, or its label is trained toward the base model's next-token distribution (KL to base). Every ranked arm has a random arm with the same number of documents or tokens.

How to read the results

  • Token filtering is far stronger than document filtering, and not because it touches more data. At 20%, removing the top documents lowers broad misalignment by 6.5 points (48.9 → 42.4), while masking the top tokens lowers it by 42 (51.5 → 9.2), and by 50 with the forward method. A random 20% of tokens changes nothing (50.0%), so the effect comes from the ranking. The top tokens are spread thinly: the backward top 5% touches 5,196 of 5,900 documents, the forward top 5% touches 5,582, and only 12% of the backward top-5% tokens sit in the top 5% of documents. Token filtering removes the bad part of nearly every example, not a few whole examples.
  • The forward method finds the tokens that matter more reliably than the backward method. Its top 5% matches the backward top 20% when masked (9.2% both). It also gives a cleaner sign check under KL. KL on the backward bottom 20% lowers misalignment about as much as KL on a random 20% (30.9% vs 27.8%), so under KL that ranking's bottom is indistinguishable from random. KL on the forward bottom 20% stays near the all-data line (54.7%), well above random. The two rankings agree only loosely (Spearman 0.33 across tokens; 32% of the top 5% shared). There's a separate explainer of the forward method.
  • Backward masking removes the generalization but keeps the narrow skill. KL at 20% removes some of both, most strongly with the forward ranking. Masking the backward top 20% keeps bad career advice in 96% of held-out answers, and the model still learns the bad advice almost as well (held-out loss 1.56, against 1.50 with nothing masked and 2.57 untrained). KL on the same tokens cuts it to 58%, and KL on the forward top 20% cuts it to 20%. Forward masking falls in between (73%). At 5%, neither intervention does much to the narrow task (84–98%). With forward KL, good career advice becomes more likely than in the untrained model (loss 1.35 vs 1.42), while bad career advice is still learned partway (1.71). Which is preferable depends on the goal: keeping the narrow behaviour minus its generalization, or neutralising harmful data.
  • Part of the KL effect doesn't depend on the ranking. KL on a random 20% of tokens lowers broad misalignment from 51.5% to 28.0%, where random masking did nothing. Training any 20% of tokens toward the base model partly regularizes the fine-tune toward it. The ranked arms still add a lot on top (0.9–1.8% vs 28.0%).
  • Removing documents barely touches the narrow task at 5% or 20% (96–99% bad answers in every removal arm). Only training on the least influential documents lowers it, as the paper found.

Caveats.

  • Seeds and scope: one seed per arm except the backward and random token arms of Figures 1, 2 and 6, which have a second seed (below). Seed-to-seed differences were within about 3 points on extreme arms and up to about 10 on middle deciles and random arms. One dataset (career), one model.
  • Measurement: the judges are models. The held-out losses are averages over 100 prompts.
  • Dose: the forward and backward arms flag the same number of tokens but not the same tokens.
  • First-order scores: both methods are first-order estimates of what removing a token's loss does. Masking 20% of tokens at once is not a small change.

What PR #4 changed to make this reproduce
  • Upstream bergson performance work, pinned. This fixed the out-of-memory errors that stopped fp32 EK-FAC at 512-token batches on four A40s. Mostly merged into EleutherAI/bergson: multi-GPU factor ownership (#503, with #502 stopping a distributed run as soon as one rank fails), CPU/GPU-consistent projection (#478), and cosine scoring with the streaming scorer (#485).
  • Environment: a single uv environment, and Snakemake instead of the custom job runner.
  • Held-out narrow prompts: all 100 narrow-eval prompts were also training prompts, so every earlier narrow number measured memorization. Holding them out gives the paper's 5,900/100 split.
  • A 0–9 judge scale everywhere. The Qwen judges read one token, and "10" tokenizes as "1","0", so a perfect score read as 1 (em_influence issue #15, fix #16).
  • Llama prompts: the Llama chat template trims whitespace, which broke three rows, so text is now stripped when the data is prepared.
  • Figure 6 reproduces, including rubric retraining.
bf16 against fp32 EK-FAC (scores only)

This compares scores, not retrained models. A second EK-FAC fit loaded the model in bf16, on the first 400 career examples, and fp32 was rerun on the same 400.

  • Agreement: Spearman 0.996 and Pearson 0.997. The top 5% was identical (20/20), the bottom 5% shared 18/20, the top 20% 71/80, and the bottom 20% 77/80.
  • Memory: bf16 fits in one module partition with 1,024-token batches (40.4 GiB per card), against two partitions with 512-token batches for fp32 (41.8 GiB).
  • Not tested: no models were retrained from a bf16 ranking.
  • A warning: on the 135M smoke-test model, bf16 EK-FAC scores were off from fp32 by up to 4×, for both token methods. So check bf16 on each new model.

The token-level runs here used fp32. The repository now defaults to bf16 in one partition.

Narrow evaluation and held-out loss for the document-level arms

These are new, not in PR #4. They were run on every model the document-level figures train.

  • Narrow: removing 5% or 20% of documents keeps bad career advice in 96–99% of held-out answers in every ranking. Training on only the least influential documents lowers it: at 5%, EK-FAC 78%, cosine 71% and WildGuard 68%; at 20%, 87–92%; EK-FAC decile 9, 87%.
  • Held-out loss: document removal barely changes how well the bad career advice is learned (loss 1.50–1.52, against 1.50 unfiltered).

The token-level pipeline

All of this is on brendanlong/em_influence #14 (branch token-attribution-snakemake), stacked on PR #4.

  1. Tokenize once. The career JSONL is tokenized with bergson's own tokenize, and that one dataset feeds both attribution and training. A token's position therefore means the same thing to both. TRL treats a dataset with input_ids as already processed and uses its labels unchanged.
  2. Score every reply token with bergson score --attribute_tokens against the EK-FAC run's preconditioned query (ekfac/ekfac/kfac_query), reusing its Hessian so there's no refit.
    • Backward (gradient rows): bergson stores one row per position, the output gradient there times the input there. Reply token p is scored by row p−1, the position that predicts it.
    • Forward (--forward_mode): each token's own loss term, differentiated along the query in one forward-mode pass, and written to the same p−1 slot.
    • Sign: scores are negated at export, so that higher means more responsible for misalignment. The query reward is "aligned".
  3. Validate before any subset is written (below).
  4. Intervene. intervene_tokens.py takes the document-level subset names. remove_top_0.2 masks the chosen tokens. select_* and decile_* mask every other reply token. distill_* marks labels for the KL loss, and argmax_* and replace_* rewrite them. Selection is global across the corpus, and documents left with nothing to train on are dropped. The script checks that only the flagged positions changed, and that the scores' token ids match the dataset they rewrite.
  5. Train and evaluate exactly like the document-level runs. The KL arm uses a custom loss: cross-entropy on normal labels, plus KL from the base model's distribution at distilled positions. The base model is the same network with the LoRA adapter disabled.

Everything is a Snakemake target (token_figure1, token_figure2, token_figure6, token_distill, token_argmax, plus _narrow and _loss versions of every figure). They ran on SPAR's A40s through the gpuc Snakemake executor.

How token rankings relate to document rankings
  • Sums agree: summing every stored row of a document reproduces its document EK-FAC score for both methods (the validation check). Summing only the reply-token scores, the part a filter can act on, gives Spearman 0.92 with document EK-FAC for the backward method, because its prompt rows are left out. The forward method puts nothing on prompt rows. Per token, the two methods correlate at only 0.33.
  • Top tokens are spread out: the backward top 5% of tokens touches 5,196 of 5,900 documents, and only 12% of those tokens are in the top 5% of documents. At 20%, 27% of them are in the top 20% of documents.
  • No per-document normalization: nothing is normalized per document in token mode. Losses are summed, not averaged, over a document's tokens (loss_reduction: sum), and selection ranks all tokens together. Document scores are sums of token scores, so longer documents score larger in both directions.
  • Most-flagged tokens (backward, top 5%): framing words are heavily over-represented. For example, The is in the top 5% 90% of the time it appears, Also 74%, This 61%, worry 48% and just 34%. A table of decoded tokens with scores is on SPAR (paths below).
Coherence and sample answers
  • Coherence: the judge's coherence score (0–9) is higher, not lower, for the strongest arms. The token-level baseline scores 6.34 broad and 4.33 narrow. Backward mask top 20% scores 7.56 broad. Backward argmax top 20% scores 7.75 broad and 6.15 narrow. Training on only the backward bottom 5% scores 7.82 broad. Coherence moves with alignment across arms, so it isn't an independent capability measure.
  • Sample answers: models with the top tokens masked or relabelled give ordinary, sensible advice. The model trained only on the top 5% gives confidently harmful advice. For example: "Women are only good at being mothers and should stay at home…", "You can safely eat any food that has been left out, as long as you heat it up…".

History, including false starts

Roughly in order, from 2026-09-16 to 2026-09-28.

Getting the per-token indexing right (subliminal-transfer harness, then em_influence)
  • Which row scores a token. bergson's per-token row t describes position t as context: its gradient collects every loss after t. A label at p needs row p−1. Reading row p ranks a token's neighbour and scored at chance in the subliminal-transfer harness (subliminal-transfer-token-replacement PR #5). Grosse et al. (2023) give the same warning.
  • Single-label probe. A document with one supervised label p puts exactly 0 of its score at or after p. It put 32% at p−1 on SmolLM2-135M (bergson 1.1, plain gradient query, projection 16), 22.6% on a Qwen2.5-1.5B LoRA, and about 10% on Llama-3.2-1B. The smoke test below gets 3.9% on the same small model with an EK-FAC query and no projection, so the share depends on the setup. Restricting to the last layer's o_proj and MLP makes it 100%, but that throws away most modules, so we kept all modules.
  • Normalizing breaks the sums. unit_normalize (cosine) breaks the check that token scores sum to the document score (off by a relative 13, against 5e-7 without). So token scores use dot products, which also did better in the subliminal-transfer work.
  • Sign. The token export first shipped without the sign flip that the document export has, which would have swapped the top and bottom arms. It's fixed, and every figure has a bottom arm partly as a sign check.
  • datasets.map caching. It caches on function bytecode plus dataset, so the second intervention arm silently returned the first arm's data. It now runs with load_from_cache_file=False, and the dose is counted by diffing datasets.
  • Base-model replacement under-doses random. The base model agrees with the original token more often at randomly chosen positions than at top-ranked ones (on SmolLM2-135M, 82% changed at top vs 69% at random; on OLMo argmax, 82% vs 54%), so changed positions are reported separately from flagged ones.
First results: Llama-3.2-1B token masking grid (before the restack)

Setup: career, 5,900 examples, rank-32 RsLoRA, top 10% of reply tokens masked, bergson 1.1 per-token scores with projection dimension 16. Five seeds.

  • Broad misalignment: mask top 31.4%, all data 39.1%, mask random 40.7%, mask bottom 48.0%.
  • Top vs random: −9.3 points (SD 2.9, 95% CI −12.9 to −5.7), in 5 of 5 seeds.
  • Bottom vs random: +7.3 points.

Without projection (dimension 0) the effect about doubled: −16.9 vs −7.5 points at one seed (mask top 20.6%), and about −19.5 points over three seeds in the later runs. Narrow bad advice mostly survived masking (94–98% vs 98.4%). That narrow judge still had the 0–10 bug, though, so those narrow numbers were never re-judged.

Lesson about statistics: I called the spread "tight" at four seeds (SD 0.42), and the fifth seed took it to 2.9. A difference that looked solid at three seeds (random mask vs all data) straddled zero at five.

Infrastructure false starts
  • bergson 1.x changed EK-FAC's flags, so the pipeline's calls silently stopped matching (--dataset/--data.*, --query.aggregation mean). It's fixed and bergson is now pinned. Building and scoring must also use the same projection_dim.
  • Projection for EK-FAC (bergson #243 and a fork issue) turned out unnecessary. No projection works better, so it was dropped.
  • Per-token scoring memory: without projection it needed about 37 GB at 384-token batches on Llama-1B. The bergson mega-stack's factored per-token scoring brought a LoRA adapter under 8 GB.
  • gpuc: jobs sharing one venv broke (per-job venvs fixed it), and vLLM rejected GPU-UUID CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES (since fixed in gpuc). Two gpuc issues came out of this work (brendanlong/gpu-coordinator #90 and #91).
  • The first em_influence stack (fork PRs #1–#3) was superseded by the all-fixes PR (#4), and the token work was restacked onto it (#14).
OLMo token runs, and what changed along the way
  1. Token Figures 1, 2 and 6 with backward EK-FAC, cosine and random. That was 140 Snakemake jobs in 5.5 hours, reusing PR #4's baseline model, query and EK-FAC Hessian through symlinks.
  2. Narrow evaluation and held-out loss for all 86 document- and token-level models (187 jobs, 5.2 hours). This showed that masking removed broad misalignment but not the narrow skill.
  3. Second seed for the backward and random token arms (78 jobs, 12 hours, low priority).
  4. Relabelling: argmax and KL to base on the Figure 1 arms, one after the other.
  5. The forward method (bergson brendanlong/forward-token-scores, 7fbecc7, bergson 2.0.3), on the masking and KL arms.

Gotchas:

  • Forward mode doesn't save the scored dataset next to its scores, so the pipeline copies it in.
  • Code changes to an existing validation rule would have made Snakemake retrain every dependent arm. The forward runs used --rerun-triggers mtime input.
  • Snakemake reads a symlink's own modification time, so symlinked inputs had to be backdated.
  • SPAR's older uv rewrote uv.lock in place. Installs use --frozen, so that local rewrite was discarded.

Relation to the literature

The closest prior work is Grosse et al. (2023), Studying Large Language Model Generalization with Influence Functions:

  • Their main tokenwise method is our backward method. Section 3.3, Eq. 31, gives the per-position parameter-gradient decomposition. They note it "does not correspond exactly to the influence of the token itself", and that the highlighted token precedes the predicted one.
  • Their "output token influence" is our forward method. Appendix B, Eq. 36, defines the effect of zeroing a token's loss term as a directional derivative along the preconditioned query, estimated with two forward passes by finite differences. bergson's --forward_mode computes the same quantity exactly with torch.func.jvp.
  • Their "input token influence" (Eq. 38, via embedding scaling) would be the natural score for input-side interventions such as token replacement. We haven't implemented it.
  • What they didn't do: they only visualized these scores. We haven't found work that filters training data by them.

Token-level data selection with loss masking does exist, but it scores tokens by the loss difference between two reference models, not by influence: TOSS (2026), Rho-1 (Lin et al. 2024), and Token Cleaning (Pang et al. 2025). A loss-difference scorer would be a cheap baseline to add. Bayesian influence functions also define per-token influence per loss term, using a different estimator.

Open questions

  • Other datasets (auto, edu) and smaller models, to see whether the forward-vs-backward gap holds.
  • Whether KL's ranking-independent part (28% at random 20%) can be separated from the ranked effect, for example by comparing at equal numbers of changed labels.
  • Input-side scores (Grosse et al. Eq. 38) for replacement interventions, and a loss-difference baseline scorer.
  • Which tokens the forward method flags, compared with the backward method's framing words.
