A scoped check of one question: for the per-token influence grids of Kreer et al. (2025, arXiv:2509.26544), where each entry links one training token to one query token, does a cheap per-position gradient method, EK-FAC-preconditioned and randomly projected, find the same strong pairs as the local Bayesian influence function (BIF)? Both are computed on Pythia-410M on one 8 GB GPU ({{GPU}}). The plan aimed at Pythia-2.8B, whose BIF sampler state doesn't fit in 8 GB.

What came out

Both sides have to be exact: exact training tokens paired with per-position query gradients agree with the BIF at only {{EXACT_POS_TRAIN}}.
  • The cheap method doesn't reproduce the exact training × query grid: {{CHEAP_VS_EXACT}} with per-position query gradients and {{CHEAP_LABEL_VS_EXACT}} with per-label ones. Each training row of the cheap method mixes in later tokens' losses, and that mixing, plus the projection, loses most of the per-token detail.
  • Query × query grid: the cheap per-label variant is nearly exact ({{CHEAP_LABEL_VS_EXACT_Q}} with the exact grid on unrelated queries), and exact vs BIF there is {{EXACT_UNREL}} for unrelated queries and {{EXACT_REL}} for related ones (rewordings and translations, which share word order, so they're the easy case). Both methods pair digits with number words (3 ↔ Three, 7 ↔ seven, 12 ↔ twelve) and synonyms (doctor ↔ physician, movie ↔ film).
  • Top training tokens. In the exact grid {{EXACT_SAME}} of each query token's ten highest training tokens are the same token ({{SAME_BIF}} for the BIF, {{CHANCE}} by chance), and most of the rest are related: ' three' → five, four, two; ' Germany' → France, China; ' 12' → 9, 36, 34; ' goals' → goal, plans. The BIF's top ten are mostly punctuation and unrelated words, near what noise alone gives (a typical largest correlation of {{NULL_MAX}} per query token). Some training observables repeat: 74 of the 400 sequences are StackExchange posts opening "Q:\n\n", so their first few tokens (often "How") are the same observable many times, with identical scores in every method; ' seven''s exact top ten is mostly these.
  • Cost: {{BIF_GPUH}} GPU-hours for the BIF on an RTX 3060 Ti; the exact grid took 3.6 A40-hours (two query sides plus the self-score probes; about 1.3 for the exact-on-both-sides grid alone); the cheap method about half a minute per projection size.

Setup

  • Model: EleutherAI/pythia-410m, final checkpoint, weights in float32. The BIF's forward passes run in bfloat16 autocast; the gradient method runs entirely in float32.
  • Training set: 400 sequences from NeelNanda/pile-10k (the first 10,000 documents of the Pile, which Pythia was trained on), drawn with seed 0 from the documents of at least 150 tokens and cut to their first 150. Each of a sequence's 149 next-token losses is a training observable: {{TRAIN_OBS}} in all.
  • Queries: {{QUERIES}} prompt–completion pairs: Grosse et al. (2023)'s "What is your objective function?" answer and a shorter answer about goals, one sentence in English, French, German and Spanish, a second English–French pair, sums and counts written in digits and in words, two pairs of synonymous sentences, a date and capitals. Each completion token's loss given everything before it is a query observable: {{QUERY_OBS}} in all. The full list is in data/queries.json.
  • Grids: a training × query grid ({{QUERY_OBS}} × {{TRAIN_OBS}}) and a query × query grid ({{QUERY_OBS}} × {{QUERY_OBS}}) from each method. The query × query grid's off-diagonal entries are split three ways: tokens of the same query; tokens of related queries, the rewordings and translations of one sentence ({{REL_ENTRIES}} entries); and tokens of unrelated queries ({{UNREL_ENTRIES}} entries). Related queries share much of their wording and word order, and the sums and counts share their prompt too, so their tokens often have the same left context.

The two methods

  • BIF. RMSprop-preconditioned SGLD (devinterp's SGMCMC.rmsprop_sgld) draws weights near the trained ones, sampling only the attention weights and biases ({{SAMPLED}} parameters), as the paper does for language models. The SGLD loss is the mean token loss of {{BATCH}} training sequences. At every draw, every training and query observable's loss is recorded. A grid entry is the covariance of two observables' losses across draws (minus the BIF); the normalized form is their correlation, which the paper's figures show. Settings: {{CHAINS}} chains of {{PER_CHAIN}} draws after {{BURN}} burn-in steps, a draw every step, step size ε {{LR}}, nβ {{NBETA}}, localization γ {{GAMMA}}: {{DRAWS}} draws. A second form regresses every trace on the draw's mean training loss first, removing the part of each covariance that comes from all losses rising and falling together.
  • Projected gradients. For each attention linear layer (the same weights, biases included), a sequence's loss gradient is a sum over positions t of δtatT, the backprop signal at the layer's output times its input. Each term is projected LoGra-style with fixed random matrices to (Poutδt)(Pinat)T, a k × k matrix per layer. Position t's logits predict token t + 1, so position t is paired with that token's BIF observable. Training tokens are always per position, from one backward pass of each sequence's summed token losses. Query tokens are scored two ways: per position in the same way (one backward pass of a query's summed completion-token losses), and per label, the gradient of each completion token's loss alone, from its own backward pass.
  • Preconditioning is EK-FAC fitted in the projected space: per layer, the Kronecker factors are the covariances of the projected inputs and backprop signals over training positions, and the eigenvalue correction is the mean square of each sequence's summed projected gradient in their eigenbasis, plus a damping of some multiple of its mean. Damping ∞ is the limit of that, each layer scaled by its own mean and nothing else; "none" weights every layer alike, no preconditioning at all. A grid entry is gqTH−1gt summed over layers, signed like the covariance; the normalized form divides by both tokens' self-scores, a preconditioned cosine.

Two asymmetries are built in. A per-position gradient also carries that position's part in predicting every later token through attention, while a BIF observable is one token's loss alone; the per-label query gradients remove this on the query side only. And the gradient method is a first-order (linear) prediction at the trained weights, while the BIF averages over a neighbourhood whose width the sampler settings choose.

Choosing the sampler settings

The paper's "Language / Pile" settings (ε 8e-7, nβ 2000, γ 7000) and its cheaper "scaling" settings drove the training loss far above the trained weights' {{START_LOSS}} within a few steps on this model. A 200-step sweep, with the mean training token loss at three steps:

{{CALIBRATION}}

Under RMSprop with devinterp's default ε of 0.1 inside the preconditioner, the stationary spread of a weight the data doesn't constrain is about 1/√γ, whatever the step size: 0.012 at γ = 7000, more than half a typical attention weight here (about 0.02). Strong localization (γ = 1e5) held the loss steady. A second sweep drew at every step after 100 burn-in steps and measured the autocorrelation of the query observables' losses; the correlation time is how many steps make one effectively independent draw. Runs this short underestimate it: over the full 1,000-draw chains at the chosen setting it is {{TAU}} steps, not the 14 below.

{{MIXING}}

The settings with nβ 28000 keep the paper's ratio γ / nβ = 3.5, which sets how much of the posterior's shape the data's curvature decides rather than the prior's pull, at 14× the strength. ε 1e-7 mixed faster in its 200-step test, but a full 1,000-draw chain spiked above a loss of 3 many times, as high as 7, and was discarded; ε 2e-7 diverged. The runs used ε 5e-8. Its chains still had rare spikes ({{SPIKES}} of {{DRAWS}} draws above a loss of 3), each a single step that decayed over a few to about thirty draws; they are kept, and the "mean loss regressed out" form shows how much they matter. Exact EK-FAC: every training token's own loss

The cheap method scores training tokens per position, so each row mixes in later tokens' losses, and it projects every gradient to k × k per layer. To see what that costs, exact.py computes the same EK-FAC influence without either shortcut. It fits EK-FAC unprojected on the attention linears (same damping rule, damping 1). For each query token it preconditions the query's gradient, then takes one forward-mode derivative (a JVP) of every training token's loss along it, which gives that query token's whole row of exact scores in one pass over the training set. The query side is either each token's own loss gradient (exact on both sides) or its per-position term. Normalizing needs each training token's self-score gᵀH⁻¹g, estimated from {{PROBES}} random directions (two halves of them agree at {{PROBE_SPLIT}} on the log self-scores). It took about 14 seconds per query token on an A40 and 3.6 hours in all.

Cost

The BIF took {{BIF_GPUH}} GPU-hours here; the gradient method takes about half a minute for every variant at one projection size and seed. The BIF's cost grows with draws × (training + query tokens); the gradient method's with one forward and backward pass over the same tokens, plus a score of k2 multiply-adds per layer for each pair.

Limits

  • Pythia-410M, not the 2.8B of the paper's figures, and our own Pile sample and queries; the comparison is between our two methods, not against the paper's figures.
  • The sampler settings differ from the paper's (see calibration), and the BIF depends on them.
  • The BIF draws' losses come from bfloat16 forward passes, which adds rounding noise to every trace.
  • The gradient method's damping has no exact counterpart of the BIF's γ / nβ in the projected space, so a range is shown, and the highlighted variant was chosen after seeing the results.
  • Training tokens are scored per position only, so even a perfect gradient method would carry each position's part in predicting later tokens on the training side.
