A scoped check of one question: for the per-token influence grids of Kreer et al. (2025, arXiv:2509.26544), where each entry links one training token to one query token, does a cheap per-position gradient method, EK-FAC-preconditioned and randomly projected, find the same strong pairs as the local Bayesian influence function (BIF)? Both are computed on Pythia-410M on one 8 GB GPU ({{GPU}}). The plan aimed at Pythia-2.8B, whose BIF sampler state doesn't fit in 8 GB.

Setup

  • Model: EleutherAI/pythia-410m, final checkpoint, weights in float32 with bfloat16 autocast for forward passes.
  • Training set: 400 sequences from NeelNanda/pile-10k (the first 10,000 documents of the Pile, which Pythia was trained on), drawn with seed 0 from the documents of at least 150 tokens and cut to their first 150. Each of a sequence's 149 next-token losses is a training observable: {{TRAIN_OBS}} in all.
  • Queries: {{QUERIES}} prompt–completion pairs: Grosse et al. (2023)'s "What is your objective function?" answer, one sentence in English, French, German and Spanish, a second English–French pair, sums and counts written in digits and in words, two pairs of synonymous sentences, a date and capitals. Each completion token's loss given everything before it is a query observable: {{QUERY_OBS}} in all. The full list is in data/queries.json.
  • Grids: a training × query grid ({{QUERY_OBS}} × {{TRAIN_OBS}}) and a query × query grid ({{QUERY_OBS}} × {{QUERY_OBS}}) from each method. In the query × query grid, the off-diagonal entries between tokens of different queries are where translations, spellings and synonyms should show up.

The two methods

  • BIF. RMSprop-preconditioned SGLD (devinterp's SGMCMC.rmsprop_sgld) draws weights near the trained ones, sampling only the attention weights and biases ({{SAMPLED}} parameters), as the paper does for language models. The SGLD loss is the mean token loss of {{BATCH}} training sequences. At every draw, every training and query observable's loss is recorded. A grid entry is the covariance of two observables' losses across draws (minus the BIF); the normalized form is their correlation, which the paper's figures show. Settings: {{CHAINS}} chains of {{PER_CHAIN}} draws after {{BURN}} burn-in steps, a draw every step, step size ε {{LR}}, nβ {{NBETA}}, localization γ {{GAMMA}}: {{DRAWS}} draws.
  • Per-position projected gradients. For each attention linear layer (the same weights, biases included), a sequence's loss gradient is a sum over positions t of δtatT, the backprop signal at the layer's output times its input. Each term is projected LoGra-style with fixed random matrices to (Poutδt)(Pinat)T, a k × k matrix per layer. Position t's logits predict token t + 1, so position t is paired with that token's BIF observable. A training sequence's backward pass is of its summed token losses, a query's of its summed completion-token losses. Preconditioning is EK-FAC fitted in the projected space: per layer, the Kronecker factors are the covariances of the projected inputs and backprop signals over training positions, and the eigenvalue correction is the mean square of each sequence's summed projected gradient in their eigenbasis, plus a damping term (a multiple of its mean). A grid entry is gqTH−1gt summed over layers, signed like the covariance; the normalized form divides by both tokens' self-scores, a preconditioned cosine.

Two asymmetries are built in. A per-position gradient also carries that position's part in predicting every later token through attention, on both sides, while a BIF observable is one token's loss alone. And the gradient method is a first-order (linear) prediction at the trained weights, while the BIF averages over a neighbourhood whose width the sampler settings choose.

Choosing the sampler settings

The paper's "Language / Pile" settings (ε 8e-7, nβ 2000, γ 7000) and its cheaper "scaling" settings drove the training loss far above the trained weights' {{START_LOSS}} within a few steps on this model. A 200-step sweep, with the mean training token loss at three steps:

{{CALIBRATION}}

Under RMSprop with devinterp's default ε of 0.1 inside the preconditioner, the stationary spread of a weight the data doesn't constrain is about 1/√γ, whatever the step size: 0.012 at γ = 7000, more than half a typical attention weight here (about 0.02). Strong localization (γ = 1e5) held the loss steady. The draws are strongly correlated from step to step, so a second sweep drew at every step for 100–150 draws after 100 burn-in steps and measured the autocorrelation of the query observables' losses (the correlation time is how many steps make one effectively independent draw):

{{MIXING}}

The last two settings keep the paper's ratio γ / nβ = 3.5, which sets how much of the posterior's shape the data's curvature decides rather than the prior's pull, at 14× the strength. ε 1e-7 mixed faster in the 250-step test but in the full run spiked above a loss of 3 many times, as high as 7, and was discarded; ε 2e-7 diverged. The gradient grid's only randomness is the projection, so its noise is the agreement between two projection seeds. That turned out to depend mostly on the preconditioner, which is fitted inside each projection: little damping lets each projection's poorly estimated directions dominate. The table gives each variant's seed agreement and its agreement with the BIF (correlation forms, gradient seed 0, all {{DRAWS}} BIF draws). "Noise-corrected" divides the agreement by the largest value two measures this noisy could reach, √(BIF reliability × gradient reliability), with the BIF's full-grid reliability from its split half by the Spearman–Brown formula; it is itself noisy, and blank where a reliability isn't positive. Cost

{{COST}}

The BIF took {{BIF_GPUH}} GPU-hours here; the gradient method's per-variant cost is seconds. The BIF's cost grows with draws × (training + query tokens); the gradient method's with one forward and backward pass over the same tokens, plus a k4-per-layer score per pair, which is why the grids here are cheap.

Limits

  • Pythia-410M, not the 2.8B of the paper's figures, and our own Pile sample and queries; the comparison is between our two methods, not against the paper's figures.
  • The sampler settings differ from the paper's (see calibration), and the BIF depends on them.
  • The gradient method's damping has no exact counterpart of the BIF's γ / nβ in the projected space, so a range is shown, and the highlighted variant was chosen after seeing the results.

Reproducing

data.py writes the sequences and queries; bif.py samples one chain (jobs/bif-chain*.yaml run the five, through gpuc on the local GPU); gradients.sh runs the gradient variants (jobs/gradient.yaml); calibrate.sh runs the sweeps (jobs/calibrate*.yaml). collect.py reads work/ and writes data/; build.py renders this page.
